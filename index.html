A Hygienist Breaks Her Silence: Why Families Keep Getting Cavities Even When They Brush Every Day

By Emma Carter, Health Correspondent  ·  Reviewed by a practising dental hygienist  ·  November 7, 2026

After 14 years in the chair, one hygienist says almost every family is quietly doing the same thing wrong, adults included.

A dental hygienist with 14 years in practice recently told us something she said she'd been holding back for years: “When someone gets a cavity, I almost never blame the person. I blame the brush. And most dentists know it.”

We kept hearing the same story. People who brush every single day, cut down on sugar, do everything they're told, and still sit in that chair hearing the word nobody wants to hear.

Cavities. Again. And it isn't just the kids. It's the parents too.

So we spent the last few weeks looking into why. We spoke to hygienists. We read the studies. And we tested the one thing they kept quietly pointing us toward. What we found genuinely surprised us, and it has nothing to do with how hard anyone is trying.

“She has four cavities.”

That's how it started for one parent we spoke to. Sitting frozen in the chair, trying to take it in.

Four cavities. In a child whose teeth they brushed themselves, every night, twice a day. And at the same visit, the parent was told two of their own teeth needed work as well.

“That's impossible,” they said. “We do everything right. Good toothpaste. Barely any sugar.”

The dentist gave them a look they said they'll never forget.

“You're not failing. But what you're doing doesn't work as well as you think. It never has, for any of us.”

Harsh. But according to the hygienist we interviewed, completely true, and it's the thing the profession rarely says out loud.

The part no one tells you

Here's what she explained, and it's backed by the research.

You can absolutely clean your teeth by hand. Nobody is saying a toothbrush does nothing. The problem is what it takes to do it properly: a full two minutes, the right small circles, the right angle at the gumline, every surface in order, twice a day, without rushing.

Almost nobody actually does that. Not tired adults at the end of the day. And certainly not a child who just wants to get to bed.

So in real life, a brush only ever reaches the easy parts. The flat front. The top. The sides, the gaps between the teeth, and the line where the tooth meets the gum, the exact places cavities start, get rushed over or missed entirely.

Studies have found that everyday brushing cleans only 27 to 30% of the tooth's surfaces. Done slowly and perfectly you can do better, but that's not how real mornings and bedtimes go.

As the hygienist put it to us:

“Most people think a cavity means they didn't brush well enough. Usually it means the brush never reached the spot in the first place. You can't fix that by scrubbing harder.”

And once you understand that, the nightly routine starts to feel pointless. You're not failing at it. You're doing a job the tool was never built to finish.
